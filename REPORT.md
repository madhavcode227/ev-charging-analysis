## The question

India's charging shortfall is usually framed as a counting problem: there are 27,737 public stations and roughly 1.32 million are needed by 2030. This analysis asks a different question -- given that chargers will be built, where should they go, and what actually determines whether they pay for themselves.


## National picture

- 27,737 registered public stations; 4,984 (18.0%) are not operational

- 235 EVs per public station, against a global benchmark band of 6-20

- 1.32 million stations needed by 2030 -- 47.6x today's base

- PM E-DRIVE has allocated Rs 2,000 crore for public charging and had spent nothing as of March 2026

## Findings


**1. The gap is real but it is the wrong target.** A large gap can mean a thick market that is underserved, or a thin market with almost no EVs. Ranking by gap alone cannot distinguish the two, and the second case produces stranded assets.


**2. Execution is weaker than sanctioning.** 18.0% of registered stations are not operational, and the PM E-DRIVE charging allocation was unspent as of March 2026. Capital is not the binding constraint.


**3. Everything turns on utilisation.** Reported real-world utilisation runs between 1% and 5%. At 2% a station never pays back; at 10% it pays back in under three years with no subsidy at all. The difference is location, not technology and not capital cost.


## Recommendations


**1. Move support from capex to delivered energy.** A per-kWh incentive pays operators for chargers that get used, which pushes site selection toward demand instead of toward whoever can install fastest.


**2. Select sites by dwell time, not by geography.** Highway rest stops, metro parking, bus depots and logistics hubs concentrate demand. Residential public charging competes with home charging, which already covers most urban four-wheeler miles, so those sites are structurally underused.


**3. Repair before expanding.** Roughly 4,984 installed chargers are not working. Restoring them is cheaper per usable charger than new installation and improves network reliability, which is what actually constrains EV adoption.


## Limitations

- Published station counts disagree by up to 2x across sources, depending on whether they count stations or points and registered or operational. One series was used throughout; absolute values are less reliable than the relative ranking.

- EV registration figures are cumulative and include two- and three-wheelers, which use public DC charging far less than cars. A category-weighted count would sharpen the demand metric.

- Weights are judgment, not fitted to an outcome. Running the score at different weights would show how stable the ranking is; that sensitivity check is not in this version.

- Tariffs, land cost and utilisation vary by city; the unit economics use one national set of assumptions.
